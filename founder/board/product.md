# Product lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **It exists and it works end to end.** A solo build covers all three sides of a service job: the customer raises it, the admin assigns it, the technician closes it. Most ideas at this stage are slides. This one can go in front of a buyer next week.
- **There is care in the parts nobody sees.** The founder measured every AI component against a baseline and wrote down the limitations without being asked. Keeping a person in the loop on every AI output is the right default when the models are unproven.
- **Two features have a clear moment of use.** "The technician talks, and the service record writes itself" and "the customer tries a fix before anyone drives out" are each one sentence long, and each saves something a small company can feel: admin time, or a wasted visit.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No focus.** Three portals, four AI models, a knowledge base, analytics and notifications, all for a company with 5 to 50 technicians. This is a feature list, not the one thing done brilliantly. A small company will not adopt a whole platform at once. Asked for in one sentence, the pitch today comes out as a paragraph.
2. **The AI is proven on the wrong world.** Urgency was trained on IT-helpdesk tickets. Failure prediction was trained on synthetic milling-machine data and needs sensor readings that home ACs, appliances and generators may not produce at all. The fix-matching and extraction tests were written by the same person who wrote the knowledge base. Nobody has seen the product's headline promise (AI that knows your equipment) work on one real Chennai job.
3. **The experience has been designed from the code outward, not from the customer's day.** No real dispatcher, technician or customer has used it. Every handoff is a gap where it can fail: a customer who has to sign up and register equipment before complaining, a technician dictating in the field with browser speech-to-text, an admin re-entering requests that arrive through other channels. Whether the customer portal beats how customers call for service today is unknown.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, what is the one thing a Chennai service owner would pay for, if everything else were cut?
- Walk me through a technician's first five minutes with it, on a real job site, in the language they actually speak at work. Does the voice capture survive that?
- How do complaints reach these companies today, and why would a customer leave that channel to sign up on a portal? (/founder-consumer)
- Which of your target equipment types produce sensor readings at all? If none do, can you cut failure prediction?
- Is there a real first company, by name, that will run one week of real jobs through it?
- What do these companies use and pay for today? (/founder-competitors, /founder-pricing)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Cut to one wedge.** Pick a single job, probably voice-to-service-record or intake-and-dispatch, and hide everything else from the first customer. Failure prediction stays out until real sensor data exists.
2. **Get one real company running real jobs.** At least a few weeks of live use with actual technicians. Record where the experience breaks at each handoff, and fix those seams before adding features.
3. **Re-measure on their data.** Rerun the urgency, fix-matching and extraction evaluations on that company's real complaints and reports, labelled by someone other than the founder, and publish the numbers next to the current ones.
4. **Write the one-sentence pitch** and test it on buyers (/founder-consumer) before choosing a price (/founder-pricing) or a budget (/founder-cfo).
